HIS LIFE

A Life Dedicated to Science

Dr. Aklilu Lemma was an Ethiopian pathologist whose scientific career became closely connected with the search for practical solutions to serious public-health problems. Born in 1935, he pursued higher education in Ethiopia and later continued his studies in the United States, where he earned a Doctor of Science degree in pathobiology from Johns Hopkins University.

After completing his studies, Lemma returned to Ethiopia and became involved in scientific research and education. He established the Institute of Pathobiology at what was then Haile Selassie I University. The institution became an important center for biological and medical research in Ethiopia and is now known as the Aklilu Lemma Institute of Pathobiology.

One of Lemma's most important scientific contributions began in 1964 when he investigated the traditional use of the endod plant, also known as soapberry. His research showed that substances from the plant could kill freshwater snails that play an important role in transmitting schistosomiasis, also known as bilharzia. This opened the way for further research into an inexpensive, locally available approach to controlling the disease.

Lemma continued to promote research into endod and its possible applications. His scientific work eventually gained international recognition. In 1989, he and his colleague Dr. Legesse Wolde-Yohannes received the Right Livelihood Award for their work on an affordable preventive approach to bilharzia. His career remains an example of how scientific research can connect local knowledge, experimentation and the search for solutions to problems affecting communities.

SCIENCE & DISCOVERY

The Endod Discovery

Endod, scientifically known as Phytolacca dodecandra, is a plant that has traditionally been used as a soap or washing agent in parts of Africa.

Dr. Lemma investigated its effects on freshwater snails involved in the transmission of schistosomiasis. His research demonstrated that compounds from the plant could act as a molluscicide, meaning a substance capable of killing mollusks such as snails.

The research became significant because controlling the snail hosts can help interrupt the transmission cycle of schistosomiasis. Lemma's work therefore connected Ethiopian plant knowledge with laboratory and field research.

“We have learned the hard way that the root problems of scientific research in Africa are not only the lack of facilities and funds but also the biases of individuals and organisations in industrialised countries.”

— Dr. Aklilu Lemma, 1989

HIS LEGACY

Why Aklilu Lemma Matters

Aklilu Lemma's legacy is not limited to one scientific discovery. His career demonstrated the value of building scientific knowledge around problems that directly affect communities.

His work brought international attention to Ethiopian scientific research and demonstrated how a locally available natural resource could become the subject of rigorous scientific investigation.

Today, his name continues to be associated with scientific education and research in Ethiopia through the Aklilu Lemma Institute of Pathobiology. His story continues to inspire students and researchers interested in using science to serve society.
